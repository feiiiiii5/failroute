\begin{abstract}
Exception handlers that turn a failure into a success-shaped value --- an LLM
judge outage becoming a legitimate \texttt{0.0} --- yield wrong answers with no
crash and no log. We call this \emph{failure-routing} and study the part a
syntactic tool can see: failures routed inside an \texttt{except} handler. A
six-rule AST detector over eight pinned AI/ML packages reports 621 findings in
the 524{,}229 lines it scans; 366 (58.9\%) lie outside the union of four
syntactic linters, which reaches 0 of the 19 \texttt{contextlib.suppress}
findings.

We then replaced the predicate ``the handler returned a constant'' with the one
the definition asks for: ``the failure became a value the caller cannot
distinguish from success, and that is not the function's contract''. Findings
fell to 476, but the share the four linters already reach \emph{rose}, 41.1\%
$\rightarrow$ 52.9\%; 155 of the 184 dropped sites were linter-invisible. Over
140 labels the split runs against us: findings no linter reaches carry 1 defect
in 56 (1.8\%, 0.3--9.4), those linters reach carry 12 in 48 (25.0\%,
14.9--38.8). The 12 that linters reach are all bare \texttt{except:}, which
\texttt{flake8}'s E722 flags in 134 candidates corpus-wide --- a search space
4.6$\times$ smaller than ours at equal recall.

The labels are weak: LLM agents, not humans, wrote them, and the frozen 12
defects are three copy-paste families, so the cluster-level rate is 4.4\%
(1.5--12.2) against the 15.0\% observation-level one. ``Maintainers fixed it, so
it was real'' also fails structurally: labelling, spontaneous repair (13/478
sites, 2.7\%) and reaction when told (16/43, 37.2\%) count differently
constituted populations --- quantified non-identifiability, not impossibility.
Recall is 3 of 14 ground-truth defects; 9 of 14 route failure outside any
handler. Such detectors are search-space reducers with human adjudication, not
oracles.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Most software failures announce themselves. An exception propagates, a process
exits, a stack trace reaches a log. The failures this paper studies do none of
this. A handler catches a timeout from a model API and returns \texttt{0.0}, and
the caller cannot distinguish that from a model that genuinely scored zero. A
decode error is caught and an empty string returned, and the code downstream sees
a document with no content rather than one that failed to load. Execution
continues, the caller receives a value of the expected type, and a wrong answer
proceeds in the shape of a right one. We call this \emph{failure-routing}: the
handler does not merely swallow the failure, it routes it into the success path.
This paper studies the subfamily a syntactic tool can see --- failures routed
inside an \texttt{except} handler.

The consequence is that the usual evidence of a defect is missing by
construction. A crash produces a bug report; a plausible wrong number rarely
produces one. Nobody files the issue, nobody writes the regression test, and the
standard warrant for a static-analysis finding --- that a maintainer read it and
fixed it --- is least available exactly where the pattern is most dangerous.
Detecting these handlers is the easy half of the problem. Establishing that what
we detected are defects is the hard half, and it is the half we do not fully
solve.

This work began with a narrower observation. A widely used Python linter
recommends a refactoring that moves the pattern outside the detection scope of
every linter we tested, including the one that recommends it.

\paragraph{The \texttt{SIM105} observation.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item \texttt{ruff}'s \texttt{SIM105} rule recommends rewriting
  \texttt{try/except/pass} into \texttt{contextlib.suppress(...)}. The two forms
  are semantically identical.
\item After the rewrite, the construct is out of reach of the linters we tested,
  and not only of the rule sets we happened to select: in our corpus all 19
  \texttt{contextlib.\allowbreak suppress} findings are invisible
  to the union of \texttt{ruff}, \texttt{bandit}, \texttt{pylint} and
  \texttt{flake8}\allowbreak\texttt{+}\allowbreak\texttt{bugbear}, and enabling
  every one of \texttt{ruff}'s 969 rules, or \texttt{pylint}'s full default message
  set, adds no rule that reports it (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}). A \texttt{semgrep}
  rule \emph{we wrote} covers 18 of the 19; citing it requires stating the rule is
  ours.
\item A widely adopted refactoring therefore moves an entire defect shape
  outside the detection scope of the tools while leaving its runtime
  consequences unchanged.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Research questions.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{RQ1} How prevalent is except-handler failure-routing in AI/ML
  Python, and how much of it do the syntactic linters practitioners already run
  reach? (Section~\ref{sec:rq1})
\item \textbf{RQ2} What fraction of the detector's findings are genuine defects,
  as opposed to deliberate contracts or tool artefacts?
  (Section~\ref{sec:rq2})
\item \textbf{RQ3} What fraction of known ground-truth defects does the detector
  reach, and how much of the phenomenon can \emph{any} handler-shaped detector
  reach? (Section~\ref{sec:rq3})
\item \textbf{RQ4} What does systematic labelling of findings do to the detector
  itself? (Section~\ref{sec:rq4})
\item \textbf{RQ5} What does replacing the detector's predicate with the criterion
  the definition actually states buy --- in findings, in coverage novelty, and in
  defects? (Section~\ref{sec:rq5})
\end{itemize}
The methodological centre of the paper sits between RQ2 and RQ3: once the answer
to RQ2 is unfavourable, what evidence could possibly settle whether the
undetected remainder is real (Section~\ref{sec:tri})? RQ5 asks the question the
first four leave open. RQ1 measures how much of the family the detector reaches
that the linters do not, and RQ2 measures how defective the detector's output is,
but neither asks whether those are the \emph{same} findings. They are not, and the
direction of the mismatch is this paper's negative result.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item A taxonomy of six failure-routing shapes and an AST detector for them
  (Section~\ref{sec:family}).
\item A reproducible prevalence and cross-tool coverage measurement over 8 pinned
  packages (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}).
\item A labelled stratified sample ($n=80$) giving a precision estimate for this
  detector family on real code (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}). The estimate is
  unfavourable to the tool, and the labelling was performed by LLM agents; both
  facts are disclosed and analysed rather than buried.
\item A tri-source triangulation for defect families where standard validation
  fails structurally (Section~\ref{sec:tri}) --- and a quantified
  non-identifiability result: its three arms count differently constituted
  populations, so no rate is common to them, and no matched-population substitute
  exists. We specify the datum that would settle the question.
\item A recall audit against maintainer-merged fixes (Section~\ref{sec:rq3})
  that doubles as a construct-validity measurement: 64.3\% of ground-truth
  defects do not route through an except-handler at all.
\item Evidence that systematic labelling is itself a detector-improvement method:
  it exposed a defect that accounted for 100\% of one rule's real-world findings
  (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}).
\item A negative result on predicate refinement, with the crosstab that produces it
  (Section~\ref{sec:rq5}). Replacing ``the handler returned a constant'' with the
  definition's own criterion cut findings from 621 to 476 and \emph{raised} the
  share four off-the-shelf linters already reach, from 41.1\% to 52.9\%. Over 140
  labels the findings no linter reaches carry 1 defect in 56 (1.8\%, Wilson
  0.3--9.4) while the findings linters do reach carry 12 in 48 (25.0\%,
  14.9--38.8). The novelty the detector claimed and the defects it found were
  largely disjoint sets, and refining the predicate widened the gap rather than
  closing it.
\item A trivial baseline for this family, which earlier versions of this paper did
  not run: \texttt{flake8 --select E722} reduces the same corpus to 134
  candidates and contains all 12 confirmed defects --- a 4.63$\times$ smaller
  reading task than the detector's 621, at equal recall on that label set
  (Section~\ref{sec:rq5}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{The pattern family}
\label{sec:family}

Six shapes, all reducible to \emph{``a failure became a value the caller cannot
distinguish from success''}, are listed in Table~\ref{tab:shapes}. The taxonomy
is defined by the semantics of the Python constructs involved rather than fitted
to the corpus studied here: four of the six shapes account for all 621 findings
reported in Section~\ref{sec:rq1}, and the two that never fire on this corpus are
retained because a shape's absence from one corpus is not evidence that the shape
does not occur. Both are exercised by the tool's unit corpus.

\centering\small
\caption{The six failure-routing shapes.}
\label{tab:shapes}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.0cm}p{5.3cm}p{6.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
Rule & Shape & Consequence \\
\midrule
\texttt{no-action} & \texttt{except ...: pass} & caller never learns the
operation failed \\
\texttt{silent-fallback} & handler returns/assigns a constant without re-raising
& judge outage becomes a legitimate \texttt{0.0} \\
\texttt{masked-exception} & catch-all conditionally re-raises \emph{and} falls

\texttt{silent-suppress} & \texttt{with contextlib.suppress(...)} & same

\section{The detector}
\label{sec:detector}

The tool is the instrument, not the subject, so we describe it only to the extent
needed to interpret the study. \texttt{failroute} 0.8.0 parses each file to an
AST and applies six rules through a shared registry, emitting text, JSON or
SARIF; individual rules can be disabled per project under
\texttt{[tool.failroute]}.

\paragraph{Unit of analysis.} One finding is one \texttt{except} handler --- or,
for the \texttt{silent-suppress} rule, one \texttt{contextlib.suppress}
statement --- reported at the line where it starts. Findings are sorted by line,
with a stable per-handler registry order for findings that share a line, so the
output order is deterministic.

\paragraph{What counts as silence.} A handler is treated as \emph{recording} the
failure, and therefore as not routing it, if its body re-raises; calls
\texttt{exit} or \texttt{\_exit}; calls \texttt{warnings.warn} or
\texttt{warnings.warn\_explicit}; or calls a logger-like name. Two properties of
that test matter for reading Section~\ref{sec:rq2}, and an earlier version of this
paragraph stated neither of them correctly.

First, the test is \emph{not} uniform across handler kinds. A \textbf{catch-all}
handler (bare \texttt{except:} or \texttt{except Exception:}) must call a method
named in \{error, warning, warn, exception, critical, fatal\}; a \textbf{typed}
handler qualifies on \emph{any} method of a logger-like object, \texttt{debug} and
\texttt{info} included --- and, because the test matches the object rather than the
method's meaning, on method names that are not logging calls at all.
\texttt{capture\_exception} on a logger-like object also qualifies. A bare
\texttt{print()} never does. So the unqualified claim this paragraph used to make
--- that \texttt{logger.debug()} does not silence a handler --- holds only for
catch-all handlers and is false for typed ones. The asymmetry is deliberate: a
caught type is itself a record of the failure its author anticipated.
Section~\ref{sec:rq5} shows the asymmetry is load-bearing, because every defect in
the frozen sample sits in a bare handler --- it falls through the strict half of
this test, not the lenient half.

Second, the test is not global. Only \texttt{silent-fallback} and
\texttt{implicit-fallback} consult it; \texttt{no-action},
\texttt{masked-exception} and \texttt{name-shadowing} report without asking whether
the handler recorded anything.

``Logger-like'' means a base-name list
(\texttt{logger}, \texttt{logging}, \texttt{log}, \texttt{\_logger},
\texttt{\_log}, \texttt{sentry\_sdk}) plus a suffix heuristic, since real
codebases name loggers \texttt{audit\_logger}, \texttt{err\_log} and
\texttt{self.\_log}. Handler bodies are traversed through control flow but
\emph{not} into nested function, lambda or class bodies, so a \texttt{return None}
inside a callback defined in a handler does not silence it.

\paragraph{What counts as a fallback value.} A fixed constant set
(\texttt{None}, \texttt{False}, \texttt{0}, \texttt{0.0}, \texttt{""},
\texttt{[]}, \texttt{\{\}}, \texttt{()}), whose ambiguous members
(\texttt{True}, \texttt{1}, \texttt{1.0}) are marked as such because they are
legitimate in boolean scorers, plus namespace-qualified sentinels such as
\texttt{Status.UNKNOWN} and any project-specific values configured under
\texttt{[tool.failroute]}. Bare names are deliberately not matched, so
\texttt{return result} can never fire.

\paragraph{Exemptions.} Three gates remove a site before any rule runs. The
first is the caught type: absorbing \texttt{KeyboardInterrupt},
\texttt{SystemExit} or \texttt{GeneratorExit} is how a process is meant to end,
and absorbing \texttt{StopIteration}, \texttt{StopAsyncIteration} or
\texttt{CancelledError} is the documented iterator and cancellation idiom, so a
handler catching only those is exempt --- and a tuple handler is exempt only when
every member is. The second is an opt-out marker in the handler's own lines:
\texttt{\# failroute: ignore} or \texttt{\# pragma: no cover}. The third is
configuration: a rule disabled under \texttt{[tool.failroute]} never fires. All
three gates are applied once, in the dispatcher, rather than re-implemented per
rule.

The heuristics deliberately err on the side of reporting: a finding is meant to
be worth a look, not to be a verdict. That design choice is part of why the
precision measured in Section~\ref{sec:rq2} is what it is.

\paragraph{Quality gates.} A 158-test suite green across a 3 OS $\times$ 5
Python-version CI matrix (Ubuntu, macOS, Windows; Python 3.9--3.13),
\texttt{mypy --strict}, and a hand-labelled unit corpus (v6 at the pinned tag,
68 samples: 36 positive / 32 negative) whose labels were written independently of
tool output, held to precision $=$ recall $= 1.0$ in CI.

\paragraph{Construct validity of the 1.0 figure.} That 1.0 is evidence the
detector matches its own specification, \textbf{not} evidence it finds real
defects. Section~\ref{sec:rq2} is the external-validity measurement, and
Section~\ref{sec:rq3} bounds how much of the phenomenon the handler-shaped
operationalisation can reach at all.

\section{Study}
\label{sec:study}

\paragraph{Statistical reporting.} Two-sided intervals are Wilson score
intervals with $z = 1.959964$; cells with a zero numerator additionally carry the
exact one-sided 95\% Clopper--Pearson upper bound $1 - \alpha^{1/n}$, and are
labelled as such wherever they appear. The recorded cells --- every interval
quoted for the labelled sample, the per-package and per-rule breakdowns, the
four-tool union, the recall audit and the triangulation --- are produced by
\texttt{tools/compute\_intervals.py} and stored in
\texttt{paper/intervals.json}; the five-tool union interval is the one figure
derived from the same formula outside that file. Several denominators below are
complete enumerations of a finite set rather than samples --- all 621 findings,
all 478 labelled tracked sites (the subset of the 2{,}128 the pipeline could label,
Section~\ref{sec:tri}), all 14 in-scope fixes, all 43 family PRs --- so their
intervals are read as \emph{superpopulation} intervals: uncertainty about the
rate in the wider AI/ML population that the corpus and the ledgers stand for, not
sampling error inside the enumeration. That wider population is not a neutral one,
and the two statements belong together: the eight packages were chosen because the
author had prior contribution history in them (Section~\ref{sec:threats}), so these
intervals quantify uncertainty around a convenience-selected corpus rather than
around AI/ML Python at large.

\subsection{RQ1 --- Prevalence and cross-tool coverage}
\label{sec:rq1}

\texttt{failroute} (v0.8.0) reports \textbf{621} findings. Baselines were run on
the \emph{same} pinned trees; Table~\ref{tab:tools} lists the tools, their
versions and the rule sets they were invoked with.

\paragraph{Matching protocol.} Each baseline was invoked with only the rules that
address this family --- \texttt{ruff} S110/S112, \texttt{bandit} B110/B112,
\texttt{pylint} W0702/W0703/W0705/W0706, \texttt{flake8} E722 with
\texttt{bugbear} B001/B017 --- and every report was reduced to a (file, line)
pair. A \texttt{failroute} finding counts as \emph{covered} by a baseline when
that baseline reported any of its selected rules in the same file within $\pm$1
line of it (\texttt{TOL $=$ 1} in \texttt{tools/coverage\_union.py}), and as
covered by the \emph{union} when any baseline covers it. Co-location is
deliberately a weak criterion --- it asks whether a tool flagged the same
handler, not whether it expressed the same property --- and the caveat at the end
of this subsection states what that costs.

\paragraph{A blind spot in our own baseline harness, found by review and fixed.}
An earlier version of this measurement passed each scan root to \texttt{pylint} as
a single directory argument. \texttt{pylint} walks a directory as a \emph{package}
and does not descend into one with no \texttt{\_\_init\_\_.py}, so every module
under such a directory is silently never analysed --- no message, no error, exit 0.
Here 152 of the 2{,}124 scanned files (7.2\%) sit under one (\texttt{inspect\_ai}
123, \texttt{deepteam} 20, \texttt{garak} 9), and RQ1's exact selection emits 34
messages across 20 of them that the directory invocation reported as zero. Handing
\texttt{pylint} the file list recovers them and perturbs nothing else: on the files
it already reached, the two invocations agree message for message (0 lost, 0
gained, per package), so the correction is a pure addition to the baseline. Every
figure in this paper uses the corrected invocation, and it moves the four-linter
union from 250 to 255 of 621 and \texttt{failroute}-only from 371 to 366 --- it
\emph{reduces} the detector's measured novelty, which is the direction a
self-inflicted error should be reported in. The other three baselines have no
equivalent blind spot: \texttt{ruff}, \texttt{bandit} and \texttt{flake8} are
filesystem walkers, and over those same 152 files their directory and
explicit-file invocations return identical hit sets (6{,}879, 104 and 2{,}375
messages respectively, both ways). The control corpus of
Section~\ref{sec:control} contains no \texttt{\_\_init\_\_}-less directory at all
(0 of 263 files), so it was never exposed. \texttt{--recursive=y} and
\texttt{--init-hook} were both tested; neither recovers a single message.

One asymmetry in that rule list we disclose rather than defend: \texttt{pylint} is
granted W0703 (broad \texttt{except}), whose direct \texttt{ruff} analogue BLE001
was \emph{not} selected, so \texttt{ruff} was the only baseline denied its
broad-except rule. Re-running with BLE001 added, \texttt{ruff}'s \emph{own}
co-located findings rise from 72 to 127 (410 findings in total rather than 76),
but the four-tool union does not move: 255 of 621 and \texttt{failroute}-only 366
under either selection, because every finding BLE001 newly reaches is one
\texttt{pylint}'s W0718 already reaches. \texttt{silent-suppress} stays at 0/19
either way, since BLE001 fires on \texttt{except} clauses and the shape in
question has none. The asymmetry therefore costs the union nothing and bears only

\paragraph{The baseline we should have run first.}
The union above is a \emph{coverage} comparison: it asks which of the detector's
findings a linter also flags. It does not ask the prior question --- how long a
candidate list one trivial rule produces, and how many of the confirmed defects
that list already contains. \texttt{flake8 --select E722} answers both, and the
answer is unfavourable to this tool. Over the same eight scan roots E722 reports
\textbf{134} bare-except sites, and all \textbf{12} defects the labelling round
confirmed (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}) are among them. The detector reports 621. A rule
that costs one flag and no semantics therefore reaches the same 12 defects from a
reading list \textbf{4.63$\times$} shorter; against the refactored detector's 476
findings (Section~\ref{sec:rq5}) the factor is \textbf{3.55$\times$}. E722's defect
yield on its own list is 12/134 $=$ 9.0\% (Wilson 5.2--15.0), a complete
enumeration, against the detector's 12/80 $=$ 15.0\% on a stratified sample that
is \emph{not} a frame estimate (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}, Method) --- so the two
yields are not directly comparable, and the comparison that matters is list length
at equal recall rather than yield.

\texttt{ruff}'s broad-except rule BLE001 is the natural companion and it does not
work here: 334 candidates, \textbf{0} of the 12 defects, one-sided 95\% upper
bound 0.9\%. BLE001 fires on \texttt{except Exception:} and not on bare
\texttt{except:}, and every defect confirmed in the frozen sample is a bare
handler. That asymmetry is the most useful single fact in this section: the
defect-bearing members of this family are the ones the \emph{least} sophisticated
rule catches and the semantically broadest one misses, which is not the ordering a
reader would predict from the coverage table above.

Both counts come from \texttt{tools/e722\_baseline.py}, which fails loudly if
either linter is unusable rather than reporting an empty hit set, and are
committed as \texttt{bench/e722-baseline.json}; the intervals come from
\texttt{tools/compute\_intervals.py --rq5}.

\paragraph{Methodological caveat on ``coverage''.}
Co-location at $\pm$1 line means a syntactic tool \emph{flags the same handler},
\textbf{not} that it expresses the same property. \texttt{pylint W0703} fires on
\texttt{except Exception:} regardless of what the handler does; it does not
distinguish ``re-raises'' from ``returns \texttt{0.0}''. 41.1\% is therefore an
\emph{upper bound} on what the syntactic tools actually say about these handlers,
and the share of findings whose \emph{property} no linter expresses is larger than
58.9\%. We report the conservative number.

Section~\ref{sec:rq5} measures what that conservatism turns out to be worth, and
the result does not favour the tool. The additional nominal novelty is concentrated
in findings that are almost never defects: over 140 labels, the findings no linter
co-locates with carry 1 defect in 56 (1.8\%), while the findings a linter does
co-locate with carry 12 in 48 (25.0\%). So the bias is real in the direction stated
--- the linters are credited with more than they say --- and its consequence runs
the other way. A larger semantic-novelty share would be a larger share of
deliberate contracts, not of defects, because the property that separates the two
in this corpus is whether the handler is bare, and bare handlers are exactly what
E722 already lists.

\subsection{RQ2 --- What fraction are real defects?}
\label{sec:rq2}

\paragraph{Method.}
Stratified random sample over (rule $\times$ package), fixed seed (20260831),
every stratum guaranteed $\geq$1 draw, remainder allocated proportionally.
$n=80$. The guarantee makes the allocation deliberately \emph{non-proportional}:
across the 19 strata of the frozen 649-finding frame the sampling fraction runs
from 8.9\% to 50\%, so the unweighted share 12/80 is a property of the sample and
not an unbiased estimate of the frame's defect share. The draw itself was taken
from the 659 frame, which carries four more strata --- the
\texttt{implicit-fallback} ones, one draw each --- and every fraction and weight
quoted here is measured against the 649 frame that survives in
\texttt{paper/scan}, because a weighted estimator mixing the two would divide by
sample items the frame no longer contains. Weighting each stratum by its frame size gives
\textbf{20.0\%} (130 expected defects in the frozen v0.7.0 finding frame);
weighting by package instead gives 19.9\%, so the granularity of the weighting does
not matter, only that the design is respected. Weighting \emph{raises} the estimate,
because the single defect-bearing stratum
(\texttt{silent-fallback} $\times$ \texttt{deepteam}, all 12 of its draws defective)
was sampled at 9.2\% while zero-defect strata were sampled at up to 50\%. We report
the unweighted shares in Table~\ref{tab:rq2}, since they are what the sample
directly shows, and the weighted or package-specific estimate wherever a population
inference is drawn (Sections~\ref{sec:tri} and~\ref{sec:threats}).
The draw is deterministic \emph{given its input frame}:
\texttt{tools/draw\_sample.py} at that seed reproduces
\texttt{paper/sample.jsonl} from the v0.7.0 finding set it was drawn from, and
selects a different sample if it is run against the v0.8.0 frame --- which is why
the script is excluded from the reproduction path (Section~\ref{sec:repro}). Each
finding was labelled by reading the surrounding source in the pinned tree into
one of three classes: \textbf{DEFECT} (the converted failure produces a wrong
caller-visible outcome with no signal), \textbf{CONTRACT} (deliberate, and either
documented, commented, or expressed in the return type), \textbf{FALSE POSITIVE}
(the detector is wrong about the code).

\paragraph{Labelling was performed by LLM agents.}
Two rounds were run, both by LLM agents (not human raters), and they agreed on
80/80 items. We do not report this as label quality, and we do not claim the two
rounds were independent: identical agreement between two runs of the same model
family is evidence of \emph{shared bias}, not of reliability, and nothing in the
artefact set demonstrates independence beyond the two runs being separate. What
is committed is both label sets --- \texttt{paper/annotations.csv} (first round,
rationales recorded in Chinese) and
\texttt{paper/annotations-second-pass.csv} (second round, rationales in English)
--- so the agreement can be checked item by item rather than taken on trust. The
sample remains useful as (a) a debugging instrument (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}) and
(b) the consequence arm of the triangulation in Section~\ref{sec:tri}, where its
limits are part of the design.

\paragraph{Sampling frame.}
The draw was taken from the \emph{v0.7.0 finding set} ($n=659$). Two subsequent
corrections changed the frame: labelling itself exposed the
\texttt{implicit-fallback} bug whose 10 findings were all false ($659
\rightarrow 649$), and the v0.8.0 precision fixes removed 28 more ($649
\rightarrow 621$, Section~\ref{sec:rq4}). Of the 80 sampled coordinates, 72
survive in the 621 set. Of the 8 deleted items, 4 --- the
\texttt{implicit-fallback} findings --- were labelled FALSE POSITIVE and 4 ---
those removed by the precision fixes --- were labelled CONTRACT;
\emph{zero labelled DEFECT was lost}. The label proportions below therefore hold
\emph{within the v0.7.0 frame}, and the surviving sample is not random with
respect to the 621 frame.

Table~\ref{tab:bypackage} by package --- the breakdown the triangulation in

The 12 defects are not 12 independent bugs. They are \textbf{three homogeneous
families} replicated across metric classes:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Silent criteria degradation (4/12).} \texttt{try:\newline \ \ self.system\_prompt
  = model.get\_system\_prompt()\newline except:\newline \ \ self.system\_prompt = \string"\string"}. A custom or
  misconfigured LLM that lacks the method silently scores every subsequent case
  under empty criteria. (This family was reported upstream by the present author
  before the labelling pass, as \texttt{confident-ai/deepteam}\#270 --- an issue,
  not a pull request. It was still open, its single comment from a non-maintainer
  and no maintainer response, when this paper was prepared. It records the author's
  own prior observation; it is not external validation of the label or of the
  tool.)
\item \textbf{Dead comparison (5/12).} \texttt{try: self.score == 1 except:
  self.success = False}. The guarded statement is a pure comparison expression
  that cannot raise, so the handler is dead code --- and \texttt{self.success} is
  never assigned on the success path.
\item \textbf{Fail-open security verdict (3/12).} \texttt{is\_vulnerable()}
  iterates results inside a \texttt{try}; on any error it returns
  \texttt{vulnerable = False}. In a red-teaming tool, ``not vulnerable'' is the
  most dangerous possible default: an infrastructure failure is converted into a
  clean security conclusion.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The defects are one handler shape, and it is the cheapest shape to lint.}
Classifying each labelled finding by the handler it sits in --- parsed from the
pinned corpus at the finding's own line, not read off the labels' prose
(\texttt{tools/compute\_intervals.py --rq5}) --- the frozen sample of 80 splits
into four strata: \textbf{13} bare \texttt{except:} handlers, of which \textbf{12
are defects} (92.3\%, Wilson 66.7--98.6); \textbf{18} catch-all typed handlers
(\texttt{except Exception:}), 0 defects, one-sided 95\% upper bound 15.3\%;
\textbf{46} narrow typed handlers, \textbf{0 defects, one-sided upper bound
6.3\%}; and \textbf{3} \texttt{contextlib.suppress} sites, 0 defects, upper bound
63.2\% --- a cell of three that is not evidence of anything and is listed only so
the strata sum to 80.

The 6.3\% is the bound that carries the argument, and it is worth saying why it is
not tighter. Pooling the catch-all typed and narrow typed handlers into a single
``typed'' stratum gives $n=64$ with 0 defects and an upper bound of 4.6\%. We do
not use it. The two strata are not equally exposed: \texttt{except Exception:} is
precisely what \texttt{ruff}'s BLE001 and \texttt{pylint}'s W0718 already fire on,
so those 18 observations are not part of the detector's uncovered contribution at
all, and folding them in buys a tighter bound by spending coverage novelty that
was never claimed. Restricted to handlers that are both typed and invisible to
those two rules, the bound is 6.3\% at $n=46$. The wider bound is the honest one.

\paragraph{What the clustering does to the 15.0\%.}
The paragraph above says the 12 defects are three families. The statistic in
Table~\ref{tab:rq2} does not know that: a Wilson interval on 12/80 treats the 80
labels as 80 independent draws, and they are not. Clustering the sample by
(package, enclosing function name) --- the key that reproduces the three families
exactly, with no judgement call about which copies belong together --- leaves
\textbf{3 defect-bearing clusters of 68}, that is \textbf{4.4\%} (Wilson
1.5--12.2), against the observation-level 15.0\% (8.8--24.4). The choice of
clustering key is not cosmetic and we report the alternatives rather than the one
that suits us: by exact signature string the same sample gives 5 of 71 $=$ 7.0\%
(3.0--15.4), because the three \texttt{\_\_init\_\_} copies differ in their
parameter lists; by (package, file, function) it gives 12 of 78 $=$ 15.4\%,
essentially unchanged, because each copy sits in its own file and that key does not
capture the replication at all. We therefore quote 15.0\% as \emph{descriptive}
--- it is what the sample contains --- and 4.4\% as the estimate the sample is
evidence for. The design-weighted 20.0\% of the Method paragraph weights strata,
not clusters, so it inherits the same dependence and corrects a different bias
(non-proportional allocation); we have no weighting that corrects both, and we keep
quoting it only where a frame-level inference is being drawn and say so there.

The pooled zero needs the same treatment. ``All but \texttt{deepteam}'': 0 of 66,
one-sided upper bound 4.4\%'' also presumes 66 independent observations; at cluster
level it is 0 of 63, upper bound \textbf{4.6\%}. The correction moves that cell by
0.2 percentage points, which is the honest size of the effect where there is little
to cluster --- the non-\texttt{deepteam} labels are not replicated the way the
defect-bearing ones are. Section~\ref{sec:rq5} adds 60 further labels for a total
of 140; at that size the cluster-level rate is 4 of 116 $=$ 3.4\% (1.3--8.5) and
the observation-level rate 13 of 140 $=$ 9.3\% (5.5--15.2).

Conversely, the 64 contracts are overwhelmingly \emph{deliberate and legible}: TUI
widget lookups that may not be mounted, optional-dependency probes, idempotent
cleanup in \texttt{finally}, and predicate functions whose
\texttt{Optional}/\texttt{bool} return type \emph{is} the contract. Many carry an
explicit comment stating the intent (e.g.\ inspect\_ai's ``fail open --- better to
skip the barrier than to hang teardown'').

Two findings in the sample are worth citing as \textbf{positive} examples of the
discipline this paper argues for: pydantic-ai's \texttt{is\_private\_ip} fails
\emph{closed} on an unparseable address (``Invalid IP address, treat as
potentially dangerous'' $\rightarrow$ \texttt{return True}), and its
\texttt{\_run\_tool} explicitly routes a caught exception onto a queue \emph{so
that the consumer re-raises it} rather than letting it vanish.

\subsection{RQ3b --- A tri-source triangulation}
\label{sec:tri}

The precision result invites the obvious rebuttal: \emph{if 85\% of findings are
not defects, the detector is noise}. The equally obvious defence --- \emph{the
defects are real, maintainers just have not noticed them} --- is exactly what a
false-positive-heavy tool would say, and on spontaneous behaviour alone it is
unfalsifiable. We triangulate three truth sources that ask \emph{orthogonal
questions} --- of, as it turns out, \emph{non-commensurable populations},
which is what bounds the conclusion below. Table~\ref{tab:arms} sets out the
three, with the ground truth each one rests on.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small

\paragraph{Ground truth behind arms 2 and 3.}
Arm 2's ground truth is a maintainer-behaviour dataset over the same eight
pinned packages, published as \texttt{paper/arm2-tracked-sites.csv}. The
pipeline behind it tracked 2{,}128 \texttt{try} sites across ten releases of
each package and labelled 478 of them FIXED/SURVIVED, discarding 1{,}650
(77.5\%) as too young (924), vanished (681) or mutated (45); none of the 13
FIXED sites traces to an author report --- the 9 PR-attributed fixes match no
author PR, and the 4 unattributed ones were cleared by package and content
check. Two properties of that population bound what arm 2 can be compared
against. Its tracking criterion (a handler carrying no raise, log or warn
signal) is weaker than the six rules of Section~\ref{sec:detector}, so only
185 of the 478 rows (38.7\%) carry a \texttt{failroute} rule at the release
where they were first observed. And its package mix is 59.4\% \texttt{deepteam},
against 22.5\% of the 621 findings. Arm 3's ground truth is the merged/closed
record of family fix PRs authored by the present author: the 16 merged members
are itemised in \texttt{paper/merged-pr-recall.csv} --- 8 of them in packages
outside the tracked eight --- and the 27 closed-unmerged members are itemised,
with per-PR closure reasons, in \texttt{paper/h1\_arm3\_classification.csv}
(copied into this artefact set from the unreleased companion repository so the
composition is third-party checkable): 19 self-closed, 1 bot-closed, 3 closed
without reaching the merits, 2 substantively accepted, and 2 adverse on the
merits. Excluding the 19 self-closures, acceptance is 16/24 $=$ 66.7\%
(46.7--82.0) at PR level --- one closure (\texttt{cvs-health/uqlm}\#427) shares
its defect substance with a counted merge (\#462) and two more are duplicates of
other submissions, so the 43 PRs overcount distinct defects (40 distinct; see
Limitation 3).

\paragraph{Verdict: directionally consistent, not established.}
The phrase ``directionally consistent'' is used in one operational sense
throughout: no arm exhibits what the strong false-positive reading requires ---
all contracts and maintainer rejection on the merits --- and the two arms that
quantify maintainer behaviour line up the same way, spontaneous repair near zero
and acceptance when told at 37.2\%. With that fixed, the natural headline
reading is arithmetic: arm 3's interval lower bound
(24.4\%) lies above arm 2's upper bound (4.6\%), the intervals are disjoint,
and disjointness is read as supporting ``nobody noticed'' over ``false
positive''. The arithmetic is right and the reading is not: what the two
denominators count is different, and neither count is warranted.

\emph{The arms are not commensurable.} Arm 2's denominator is 478 tracked sites;
arm 3's is 43 defect candidates pre-screened by the author, of whose 16 merged
members 8 lie in packages outside the tracked eight. A site-level rate and a
self-selected-candidate rate answer different questions about different
populations. The gap is not only one of selection: the tracked-site criterion is
weaker than the detector's, so 61.3\% of arm 2's rows carry no \texttt{failroute}
rule at all, while arm 1's defect share is measured on findings. No interval
comparison across the three arms can support a population-level verdict.

\emph{The composition axis, resolved.} Whether the intervals are disjoint depends
on two quantities: $n$, the number of tracked sites that are genuine defects, and
$k \le 13$, the number of the 13 spontaneous fixes that landed on defect sites.
$k{=}13$ is the binding corner, since a smaller $k$ only lowers the arm-2 rate.
For $n$, an earlier version of this paper rebased arm 2 with its own unweighted
15.0\% share ($n \approx 72$), obtained 13/72 $=$ 18.1\% (10.87--28.48), found
that this \emph{overlaps} arm 3, and withdrew the verdict on that basis. That
rebasing does not survive scrutiny, for two reasons that point the same way. The
15.0\% is an unweighted sample share from a design that allocates deliberately
unequally --- every stratum is guaranteed one draw and the sampling fraction runs
8.9\% to 50\% --- so it is not an unbiased estimate of the frame's defect share;
weighting by that design gives 20.0\% (each rule $\times$ package stratum weighted
by its frame size) or 19.9\% (weighting by package), so the granularity does not
matter, only that the design is respected. And a global share of any kind is the
wrong shape for this corpus:
Table~\ref{tab:bypackage} places all 12 defects in \texttt{deepteam}, while the
478 tracked sites are 59.4\% \texttt{deepteam} against 22.5\% of the 621 findings.
Applying \texttt{deepteam}'s own labelled rate (12/14 $=$ 85.7\%) to its 284
tracked sites gives $n \approx 243$; its Wilson lower bound alone gives
$n \ge 171$. Table~\ref{tab:sens} crosses that axis with the risk-set convention.
In 10 of the 12 cells the intervals remain disjoint, and disjointness needs only
$n \ge 86$, i.e.\ 18.0\% of the 478. \textbf{The two cells that overlap are both
the unweighted 15.0\% share applied to a near-complete risk set.} The composition
axis therefore does not leave the verdict undecidable; it decides it in the
direction of the reported arithmetic.

\emph{Why no verdict follows anyway.} Every cell of Table~\ref{tab:sens} answers
a counterfactual --- what arm 2's rate would be \emph{if} the tracked sites had
the defect composition of the findings. That transfer is not merely unmeasured,
it is unsupported: the two populations are not the same objects, and Limitation 2
says $n$ cannot be measured from spontaneous behaviour at all, which is precisely
the family's defining problem. The second degree of freedom is weaker still.
Which tracked sites belong in the risk set is a convention this paper did not
state: the 478 are the sites the pipeline could label, and 1{,}650 of 2{,}128
(77.5\%) were discarded. Widening the risk set only widens the gap
(Table~\ref{tab:sens}, third and fourth blocks), but the numerator convention
does not follow. Counting every vanished site as maintainer action puts arm 2 at
694/1204 $=$ 57.6\% (54.83--60.40), entirely \emph{above} arm 3's 24.4--52.1 ---
maintainers repairing more of these sites when nobody asks than when asked. That
reading is not credible, and nothing in the data excludes it. The comparison is
therefore not delicate but ill-posed, and we report the directional agreement
without a verdict.

\emph{No commensurable substitute exists.} The natural repair --- compare only
sites that are both tracked and PR-reported --- is empty: none of the 16 merged
family fixes maps to any of the 478 tracked sites (zero exact file matches;
the tracked population and the author-reported population are near-disjoint, a
consequence of the recall audit's 3 of 14 in-scope detections and of the 8
out-of-corpus fixes). The closest
available approximation is package-level: in \texttt{uqlm}, 0 of 12 tracked
family sites were spontaneously fixed through the tracked window (Wilson 95\%
0.0--24.2; exact one-sided upper bound 22.1) while 5 of 5 author-reported
\texttt{uqlm} family fixes were merged (Wilson 95\% 56.6--100.0). Same
maintainer team --- but different site sets, author-selected, and $n$ too small
to exclude almost anything. We report it as context, not as a test.

\emph{What survives.} Three things, each weaker than a verdict.

(i)~Directly, the itemised closure record
(\texttt{paper/h1\_arm3\_classification.csv}) shows that maintainer engagement
with surfaced family defects was mostly, but not uniformly, favourable. Of the 24
non-self-closed outcomes --- 16 merged, 7 maintainer-closed, 1 bot-closed --- the
16 merges and two closures accepted the substance: the gap reported in
\texttt{Giskard-AI/giskard-oss}\#2614 was fixed upstream, though not by the pull
request the ledger names --- \#2623, a third party's restatement of the same
change, was itself closed unmerged, and the change landed as \#2647 (merged
2026-08-03, described in its own body as a formatted follow-up of \#2623); and
\texttt{cvs-health/uqlm}\#427 was closed on process
grounds with its substance partially re-landed via merged \#462. Three never
reached the merits: two AI-assistance-policy closures of the same
\texttt{HypothesisWorks/hypothesis} diff, and one duplicate. And
\textbf{two were adverse on the merits}. In \texttt{cvs-health/uqlm}\#452 the
maintainer questioned the reachability of the failure and the question was never
resolved --- the closest thing in the record to a dispute of the defect's
existence. \texttt{vllm-project/vllm-metal}\#541 was declined as
speculative hardening rather than a documented gap. The direct evidence against
the \emph{strong} false-positive reading is therefore real but mixed:
favourable in 18 of the 24 outcomes at PR level, adverse on the merits in 2,
non-merits or non-maintainer in 4 --- and all of it on an author-selected subset.

(ii)~Spontaneous repair of tracked family sites is rare (2.7\%) whatever their
composition --- but by Limitation 2 that rarity is equally consistent with
``mostly contracts''.

(iii)~The datum that would settle ``nobody noticed'' against ``false positive''
--- maintainer reaction to a \emph{random} sample of findings --- does not exist
and cannot be retro-constructed. We state the question as open and record that
datum as a design requirement for future work.

\paragraph{The five limitations, stated with the result.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item Arm 1 is LLM-judged (two rounds, same model family, 80/80 agreement $=$
  shared bias, not quality).
\item Arm 2's SURVIVED label is only readable as ``nobody acted'', never as
  ``deliberate contract'' --- for this family the two are indistinguishable,
  which is precisely why spontaneous behaviour cannot validate the detector.
\item Arm 3's selection bias is irreducible: acceptance applies only to the
  self-selected reports, not to the detector's findings at large.
\item The arms are not commensurable: arm 2 counts tracked sites, arm 3 counts
  author-selected defect candidates, and 8 of the 16 merged candidates lie
  outside the tracked packages. No interval comparison between them yields a
  population-level verdict (Verdict, above; Table~\ref{tab:sens}).
\item Arm 2's denominator is a convention rather than a measurement: 478 of the
  2{,}128 sites the pipeline tracked, the other 1{,}650 discarded as too young,
  vanished or mutated; and only 38.7\% of the 478 carry a \texttt{failroute} rule,
  so arm 1's finding-level defect share does not transfer to them. Neither the
  exclusion boundary nor the composition of what it retains is measured
  (Table~\ref{tab:sens}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{RQ4 --- Labelling as a detector-improvement method}
\label{sec:rq4}

All four false positives in the sample were \texttt{implicit-fallback}, and all
four had the \emph{same} root cause:
\texttt{\_has\_top\_level\_terminal()} inspected only \emph{direct} statements of
the handler body, so a \texttt{return}/\texttt{raise} nested inside an exhaustive
\texttt{if/else} was invisible. Table~\ref{tab:fpshapes} lists the four real
shapes that triggered it.

We implemented a recursive exhaustiveness analysis
(\texttt{\_always\_terminates}) covering \texttt{if/else}, \texttt{with},
\texttt{try/except/finally} and \texttt{match}, added six regression tests derived
from the four real shapes (each failing before the fix), and re-ran the corpus.

\textbf{\texttt{implicit-fallback} findings on the 524{,}229 lines the detector
actually reads: 10 $\rightarrow$ 0.
Total findings: 659 $\rightarrow$ 649 (v0.7.0).} The v0.8.0 precision release
removed 28 more findings through four audited fixes (tuple-typed handlers,
\texttt{warnings.warn} recognised as a signal, \texttt{StopAsyncIteration}
added to the ignore list, conservative optional-dependency probe):
\textbf{649 $\rightarrow$ 621}, with every deletion traceable to a labelled
category or a mechanical criterion --- no labelled DEFECT was removed. The test
suite grew over the same period, from 124 to 130 (the six regression tests of
this subsection) and then to \textbf{158} (twenty-eight more with the v0.8.0
precision release).

Two things follow. First, the rule's entire real-world yield was false. Second,
\textbf{a stratified labelling pass is not only an evaluation instrument but the
only method we applied that exposed a defect in our own detector.} The unit
corpus (68 samples, precision $=$ recall $= 1.0$) never exposed this bug, because
the corpus was written by the same person, with the same blind spot, as the
detector.

\subsection{RQ5 --- Refining the predicate, and what it bought}
\label{sec:rq5}

\paragraph{Provenance, stated first because it bounds everything below.}
The detector in Sections~\ref{sec:detector} to~\ref{sec:rq4} is \texttt{failroute}
0.8.0: released on PyPI, pinned by the tag \texttt{v0.8.0}, installable by a
reader. The detector in this subsection is a later one whose core predicate was
rewritten, and it is now released on the same terms: \texttt{failroute} 0.9.1 on
PyPI, pinned by the tag \texttt{v0.9.1}. Drafts of this paper written before that
release described it as existing only in the authors' working tree; that is no
longer the case, and the sentence is corrected here rather than deleted because
the artefacts under \texttt{bench/} were produced before the release. Re-running
the released build over the pinned corpus reproduces Result~1's 476 findings
finding-for-finding against the committed export \texttt{bench/rescan-v2/}
(re-verified 2026-09-07). What bounds the rest of this subsection is therefore not
provenance but design: the refactor was developed against the same corpus and the
same 140 labels it is measured on, with no held-out set. We say so at the top
rather than in Section~\ref{sec:threats} because it determines the weight the
numbers below can bear.

\paragraph{What changed.} v0.8.0's \texttt{silent-fallback} rule asks whether the
handler's body returns or assigns a member of a fixed constant set. That is a proxy
for the definition in Section~\ref{sec:family}, which asks something narrower:
whether the failure became a value the caller cannot distinguish from success,
\emph{and} whether that conversion is the function's own contract. The refactor
replaces the constant test with three layers. \textbf{(1) Value-range membership:}
if the enclosing function's declared return type admits the substituted value, the
substitution is distinguishable by declaration and the finding is downgraded ---
applied only to the return channel and only to explicitly declared ranges, never to
an assignment into object state. \textbf{(2) Exception--predicate isomorphism:}
whether the handler's work is structurally the same as a \texttt{-> bool} predicate
the codebase already declares. \textbf{(3) Data-flow consequence:} whether the
substituted value escapes the call (returned) or outlives it (assigned to object
state), which is what makes a substitution caller-visible. Every finding now also
carries a severity (INFO, LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH), an isomorphism class, and a static
\texttt{covered\_by} list naming the lint rules that already flag this handler. That
list is a table derived by running the four baselines over generated probes rather than
by reading rule names
(\texttt{tools/lint\_mapping\_probe.py} $\rightarrow$
\texttt{bench/lint-rule-mapping.json}); an earlier version inferred it from the
handler's shape alone and got three of its conditions wrong, which Result 2 below
reports and corrects.

\paragraph{Result 1: fewer findings, and more of them already linted.}
Findings fall from 621 to \textbf{476} (617 to 472 distinct handler sites; four
sites are reported twice in \emph{each} frame, because the assign channel emits one
finding per assigned target while the finding identifier is derived from the handler
alone --- an identifier collision in the refactored detector, noted rather than
hidden). The four-linter union's coverage of those findings \textbf{rises} from
255/621 $=$ 41.1\% to 252/476 $=$ \textbf{52.9\%} (Wilson 48.5--57.4), and
\texttt{failroute}-only falls from 366 (58.9\%) to \textbf{224} (47.1\%,
42.6--51.5).

The refactor is not a pruning of the old frame, and treating it as one would
overstate its coherence. Over distinct sites, \textbf{184 disappeared, 39 appeared
and 433 persisted}. Of the 184 dropped sites, 155 (84.2\%) had been
\texttt{failroute}-only; of the 39 added, 15 (38.5\%) were. The losses concentrate
in \texttt{silent-fallback} (177 of 184, plus 4 \texttt{no-action} and 3
\texttt{silent-suppress}); the additions are all \texttt{silent-fallback}.
\texttt{silent-suppress} falls from 19 findings to 16, so RQ1's ``0 of the 19'' is
a property of v0.8.0 and does not carry over to this frame. In short the
intervention removed linter-invisible findings preferentially and added
linter-visible ones --- the opposite of what sharpening a predicate is supposed to
do if the thing being sharpened is novelty.

RQ2 measures how defective the detector's output is. Neither asks whether those are
the same findings. Joining all 140 labels to the refactored frame on (package, file,

sample was reclassified from \texttt{implicit-fallback} to \texttt{silent-fallback} ---
104 join and 36 do not; all 36 come from the frozen 80 and are 33
CONTRACT plus 3 FALSE POSITIVE, so \textbf{no labelled defect was lost}, and only

\paragraph{The static \texttt{covered\_by} field was unsound. We measured the whole
mapping and fixed it.}
The inference credited \texttt{bandit:B110} and \texttt{ruff:S110} to every handler
with an inert body, without looking at what the handler catches. Both rules are
broad-only. In the state an earlier version of this paper described, the faulty
inference called 87 of 476 findings (18.3\%) novel against co-location's 224 (47.1\%),
and 137 findings it called statically covered were flagged by no linter at all. One of
them is \texttt{garak/analyze/ci\_calculator.py:40}, whose handler is a literal
\texttt{except (ValueError, TypeError): pass}. Running all four baselines on that
single file returns \emph{nothing} --- \texttt{ruff} reports ``All checks passed!'',
\texttt{bandit} and \texttt{flake8} return no rows, \texttt{pylint} scores the file
10.00/10. This is a defect in our own instrument, it errs in the direction that
flatters the instrument, and we report it as a finding of the study rather than
quietly repairing it.

We did not patch the witness; we measured the mapping.
\texttt{tools/lint\_mapping\_probe.py} generates 68 probes --- eleven caught-type forms
(bare; \texttt{Exception}; \texttt{BaseException}; \texttt{builtins.Exception}; a tuple
of two narrow names; a tuple mixing \texttt{Exception} with a narrow name, with a dotted
name, and with a name no resolver can read; a single narrow name; a dotted narrow name;
and a caught type that is a call expression) crossed with six body forms
(\texttt{pass}, \texttt{...}, \texttt{continue}, a returned constant, an assigned
constant, a discarded comparison), plus both \texttt{contextlib.suppress} forms ---
runs all four baselines over them, attributes every hit to the case whose function span
contains it, and writes the matrix to \texttt{bench/lint-rule-mapping.json}. Every rule
carries a positive control, so a selector that silently matches nothing fails the run
instead of being recorded as ``never fires''. That guard paid for itself twice.
\texttt{ruff} rejects an unknown selector with exit code 2 rather than an empty result
set. And \texttt{pylint} accepts \texttt{W0703} --- the retired id RQ1's baseline
enables --- and reports those messages as \texttt{W0718}: enabling only \texttt{W0703}
emits \texttt{W0718} and nothing else, and over the corpus RQ1's exact selection emits
423 \texttt{W0718}, 134 \texttt{W0702} and 6 \texttt{W0706}, which is the 563 of
Table~\ref{tab:tools}. A reproducer who counts \texttt{W0703} messages finds zero and
would reasonably conclude the broad-except rule never ran.

The measurement found \emph{three} wrong conditions, not the one we started from:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Caught type, ruff.} \texttt{S110} and \texttt{S112} fire when the handler
  is bare or catches \texttt{Exception}\,/\allowbreak\,\texttt{BaseException}, including
  inside a tuple of any composition.
\item \textbf{Caught type, bandit.} \texttt{B110} and \texttt{B112} are narrower still:
  bare, or \emph{exactly} \texttt{except Exception:}. They do not fire on
  \texttt{except BaseException:}, on \texttt{except builtins.Exception:}, or on any
  tuple. Gating all four rules on one ``broad'' predicate --- the obvious fix --- would
  have moved the over-credit rather than removed it, and left
  \texttt{pydantic\_ai/\_agent\_graph.py:205}, an
  \texttt{except BaseException: pass}, wrongly credited.
\item \textbf{Body form.} \texttt{S110} and \texttt{B110} are literally
  ``try-except-\emph{pass}'': they fire on a body of \texttt{pass} and not on a body of
  \texttt{...}, even for a bare handler. This detector's own notion of an inert body
  accepts both forms, so the distinction had to be made in the mapping rather than
  inherited from that flag. On this corpus that third condition changes no number ---
  all 146 over-credited findings have a \texttt{pass} body --- but it is a real defect
  and the probe pins it.
\end{itemize}

Correcting the caught-type conditions then exposed an error in the \emph{opposite}
direction, which is the argument for measuring rather than patching. The predicate
deciding whether a handler catches \texttt{Exception} gave up entirely when any member
of a caught tuple was not statically resolvable, so
\texttt{except (anyio.get\_cancelled\_exc\_class(), Exception):} --- three instances in
\texttt{inspect\_ai} --- was classified as narrow and kept only its body-form credit
(\texttt{bandit:B110}, \texttt{ruff:S110}), losing the two caught-type credits that
do fire on it (\texttt{pylint:W0718}, \texttt{ruff:BLE001}); and \texttt{bandit:B110}
should not have been there either, since B110 fires on no tuple at all. That error
over-states novelty. Leaving it in place would have made the corrected figure look
better for the tool than the truth.

The correction is held by 36 tests, and a suite that passes proves nothing about
itself, so \texttt{tools/refix\_replay.py} rebuilds the pre-correction predicate
in a scratch tree and runs the same 36 against it: \textbf{21 fail}. Red before
the change and green after it, on identical tests --- the suite constrains the
mapping rather than accompanying it.

\textbf{Before and after.} The correction removes credit from \textbf{146} findings and
adds it to 3. Of the 146, \textbf{137} become statically novel and 9 stay covered
because a rule that genuinely fires on them is still credited. All 146 are
\texttt{no-action} findings --- 93 at INFO, 48 at MEDIUM and \textbf{5 at HIGH}.
Statically novel findings go from 87 (18.3\%) to \textbf{224 (47.1\%)}, and HIGH
findings with no lint coverage from 20 to \textbf{25}. What the detector \emph{reports}
does not change at all --- 476 findings before and after --- and neither do the
empirical co-location figures (252 covered, 224 novel), because those come from running
the linters rather than from inferring anything about them. That the statically novel
count and the empirically novel one are now the same number is not a coincidence; see
the next paragraph.
\texttt{tools/rq5\_refactor\_delta.py} recomputes the whole comparison from the
preserved pre-fix export; \texttt{tools/lint\_mapping\_probe.py --check-parity}
re-derives the mapping and fails if the shipped inference drifts from it; and 36 tests
pin the same conditions, including the reverse direction (a narrow handler must lose the
credit, and widening it to bare must restore it).

\paragraph{A residual we had not eliminated --- and then did.}
Five findings of the 476 disagreed with the empirical measure. All five are
\texttt{except Exception:} handlers that return or assign a constant; the corrected
inference credits \texttt{pylint:W0718} and \texttt{ruff:BLE001}, \texttt{BLE001}
does fire at exactly that line, and yet the co-location run recorded no linter there
at all. Three explanations were tested and ruled out: it is not a parse failure
(\texttt{pylint} rates \texttt{model/\_providers/openai.py} 9.96/10 with no fatal or
syntax message, and the file's single \texttt{except Exception} is the one it
declines to flag); it is not an inline pragma (none of the four files contains the
string ``pylint''); and it is not the \texttt{as} binding, which real code uses and
the probe originally did not --- tested directly, \texttt{W0718} fires on
\texttt{except Exception:}, on \texttt{except Exception as e:} and on the same
handler that uses \texttt{e}, and does not fire on a bare \texttt{except:}. The
synthetic case that matches these five \emph{does} earn \texttt{W0718} in the probe,
so the discriminating condition was something about their context. It was our
harness, not the linter: all four files sit under a directory with no
\texttt{\_\_init\_\_.py}, and the directory invocation of \texttt{pylint} that
Section~\ref{sec:rq1} describes and retracts never analysed them. Handed the files
directly, \texttt{pylint} emits \texttt{W0718} at the disputed line in every one of
the five. Two corrections follow, and the second reverses a direction this paper
previously stated. First, the residual is empty: with the harness fixed, the
detector's static \texttt{covered\_by} and the empirical co-location agree on
\textbf{all 476 findings}, not merely on the 104 joined labels --- 224 statically
novel and 224 empirically novel, and they are the same 224. Second, the earlier
claim that the residual ``over-credits the linters and understates our novelty'' was
backwards. \texttt{W0718} is inside RQ1's own \texttt{pylint} selection and it does
fire on all five, so the empirical measure was \emph{under}-crediting the linters
and the reported \texttt{failroute}-only count was \emph{over}-stating our novelty
by five findings in 476: 229 as previously reported, 224 as measured now. None of
the five was a labelled finding, which is why the crosstab in
Table~\ref{tab:crosstab} never moved --- it read 12 of 48 against 1 of 56 before the
correction and reads exactly the same after it.

Two consequences for the argument. First, the corrected inference and the empirical one
now agree on every finding in the frame, which is why the two blocks of
Table~\ref{tab:crosstab} are identical and why no residual is left to bound. Second,
and it is why the \texttt{covered\_by} fix was worth making rather than merely
documenting: the five additional uncovered HIGH findings it produced are two
\texttt{except SyntaxError: pass}, one \texttt{except FileNotFoundError: pass}, one
\texttt{except (OSError, \_7Z\_ARCHIVE\_ERROR): pass} and one
\texttt{except anyio.get\_cancelled\_exc\_class(): pass} --- all narrow-typed, and none
in \texttt{deepteam}. In the labelled sample narrow-typed handlers carry 0 defects in 46
frozen draws (one-sided upper bound 6.3\%, Section~\ref{sec:rq2}) and 1 in 23
supplementary draws, and that one is the single defect already sitting in the novel
cell, which we decline to generalise from below. So the correction multiplies the
nominally novel set by 2.6 and the part of it that commands attention by five, without
adding any defect evidence. The central comparison survives it unchanged: 12 of 48
against 1 of 56.

\paragraph{Result 3: the layer built for this did nothing.} Of the 476 findings the
isomorphism layer classified \textbf{none} as isomorphic: 254 non-isomorphic, 206
unknown, and 16 with no class at all (the \texttt{silent-suppress} findings, which
have no handler). A layer that never produces a positive classification contributed
nothing to the separation measured above; whatever the refactor achieved came from
layer 1 (declared value ranges) and layer 3 (escape channel). Severities across the
frame: 208 HIGH, 126 MEDIUM, 129 INFO, 13 LOW.

\paragraph{Result 4: what the refactor did accomplish.} All 13 labelled defects
survive it, and all 13 sit at HIGH --- no defect was lost or downgraded. Of the 122
CONTRACT labels, 33 were dropped and 25 reduced to INFO or LOW: 58 of 122, 47.5\%.
Restricting to the case layer 1 was built for, contracts whose enclosing function
\emph{declares} a None-admitting return type, 35 of 38 (92.1\%) were dropped or
reduced; for a literal \texttt{-> None}, 22 of 23 (95.7\%). Not all of them, and the
exceptions are the interesting part: three declared-contract findings still report
at HIGH or MEDIUM, and \textbf{two} labelled FALSE POSITIVES survive at HIGH. One is
\texttt{pydantic\_ai/ui/\_event\_stream.py:293}, an async generator whose handler
propagates the error elaborately and which the refactor reports \emph{twice}. The
other is \texttt{fickling/polyglot.py:150}, which the frozen sample labelled as an
\texttt{implicit-fallback} over-call and the refactor now reports as
\texttt{silent-fallback} instead, still at HIGH. The second one is visible only
because the join in Result 2 keys on the handler's coordinates and not on the rule
name: a rule-inclusive join counts a reclassified handler as a deletion and quietly
reports one surviving false positive instead of two. And 55
of the 122 CONTRACT labels sit in functions with no declared return annotation at
all, so layer 1 cannot apply to them by construction. That is the ceiling on how
much noise this design can remove: without type information, the declared-contract
test has nothing to read.

\paragraph{The 60 supplementary labels.} RQ2's 80 labels were drawn from the

rationale, the enclosing signature, and a flag for whether the site is new in this
frame. One row is marked in its own rationale as the weakest of the 60 --- a

than being quietly resolved. The outcome was 1 DEFECT, 58 CONTRACT, 1 FALSE
POSITIVE. Labels were written by an LLM agent, the same process as RQ2's, and
inherit every limitation RQ2 states about it, including that agreement between runs
of one model family is shared bias rather than reliability; we did not run the 60
twice. The 60 are \emph{not} a random sample, so the 140-label figures support a
within-sample comparison --- covered against uncovered --- and no wider inference.

\paragraph{The one defect in the novel remainder.} The single DEFECT among the 56

handler labelled in the supplementary round. We record it because the cell is 1 and

A single observation is not a measured advantage, and it is not the increment RQ1's

remainder is not empty of defects, so the honest claim is that it is
\emph{sparsely} populated with them (1.8\%, upper bound 9.4\%), not that it contains
none.

\subsection{Control-corpus comparison}
\label{sec:control}

\paragraph{Design.} The main corpus is dominated by one handler shape: 134 bare
\texttt{except:} clauses, 131 of them in a single package, holding all 12 confirmed
defects. Any claim of the form ``the lint baseline already covers the defects''
may therefore be an artefact of code style rather than a general fact. The control
corpus inverts the style: across its eight package bodies (263 \texttt{.py} files,
109{,}400 lines, 842 handlers) there is exactly one bare \texttt{except}, in
\texttt{msgpack/fallback.py:812}, whose handler resets the buffer and re-raises
--- so even the single E722 candidate is a non-defect. The detector build is the
frozen 0.9.x predicate, unmodified; the four baselines run with RQ1's exact rule
selection and $\pm$1-line matching. The four first packages contribute 243 handlers
and 0 bare clauses; the four added for domain diversity contribute 599 handlers
and the 1 re-raising bare clause.

\paragraph{Result: density does not collapse, E722 does.} Table~\ref{tab:control}
contrasts the two styles. Finding density is 21.0\% (476/2{,}270) against 15.6\%
(38/243) on the first four control packages and 18.3\% (154/842) on all eight:
no order-of-magnitude gap, so ``the tool reports less on disciplined code'' is
not the story. The story is the trivial baseline. \texttt{flake8 --select E722}
returns 134 candidates on the main corpus, containing all 12 confirmed defects;
on the control corpus it returns 1 candidate, containing none, for a recall of
zero. A two-proportion $z$ test of Group~A against the four-package control core
(476/2{,}270 vs 38/243) gives $z=1.96$, $p=0.050$, on the significance boundary;
against all eight control packages (154/842) it gives $z=1.65$, $p=0.098$, and is
not. We name the pair because the choice decides which reading applies, and we
therefore state neither ``constant'' nor ``different'': no comparison here is
statistically significant, and the sample is too small to assert equality.

\paragraph{Result: the 38 control findings contain no defect.} All 38 findings on
the first four packages were adjudicated handler by handler (file, enclosing
function, all read sites; the two HIGH findings additionally against repository
history, closed-issue records and pinning unit tests). Zero are defects, which
bounds the defect share at 7.6\% one-sided 95\% Clopper--Pearson --- against
77.6\% had only the 2 unique-HIGH findings been read. The 2 unique HIGH findings
are both documented contracts. \texttt{attrs/src/attr/\_compat.py:51} catches
\texttt{(ValueError, TypeError)} when \texttt{inspect.signature} fails and stores
\texttt{None}; the class docstring states it returns \texttt{None} ``whenever
there is none'', both read sites guard on the field, the shape dates to 2022
(\#912), and a unit test pins the extractor's behaviour. No closed issue reports
it swallowing a failure. \texttt{urllib3/src/urllib3/connectionpool.py:812}
catches transport errors, wraps each into a \texttt{ProtocolError}, feeds it to
\texttt{retries.increment} --- which raises \texttt{MaxRetryError\ldots from reason}
on exhaustion (\texttt{util/retry.py:543}) --- saves it for the retry warning,
closes the connection in \texttt{finally}, and recurses into \texttt{urlopen};
the exception is never swallowed. That control-flow structure dates to 2009 with
a 2016 revision, and the retry-exhaustion tests assert the \texttt{MaxRetryError}
propagation. The remaining 36 findings are the same contract shapes as the main
corpus's benign tail: optional-dependency and codec probes, \texttt{try/except/else}
sentinels, re-raises, and teardown handlers.

\paragraph{Result: the recall side is exhaustively clean.} Of the control corpus's
243 handlers the tool reports 38; 114 unreported handlers contain a \texttt{raise}
and need no review; the remaining 91 silent-and-unreported handlers were each
read. Zero are misses the tool should have caught --- optional-import and
capability probes, \texttt{StopIteration} protocol handling, \texttt{dict.get} and
\texttt{list.index} sentinels, documented best-effort fallbacks
(\texttt{requote\_uri}, \texttt{get\_unicode\_from\_response}), data-model protocol
returns (\texttt{NotImplemented}), and CLI exit paths. Exhaustion means
exhaustion: 91 of 91 with zero misses is a true zero-leak statement, not an
interval. The 15 prior pilot draws this replaces had a residual bound of 18.1\%.

\paragraph{The four diversity packages agree.} \texttt{click} (31 findings, 13
HIGH), \texttt{anyio} (45 findings, 1 HIGH), \texttt{pydantic} (40 findings, 1
HIGH) and \texttt{msgpack} (0 findings) reproduce the pattern: 12 of the 15 HIGH
findings are \texttt{except Exception} capability probes already covered by
\texttt{BLE001}/\texttt{W0718} (Windows-console shims, stream predicates,
\texttt{TaskGroup} propagation with explicit re-raise paths). The 3 uncovered
HIGH findings are contracts on reading: \texttt{click/core.py:161} is a
\texttt{list.index} probe returning infinity as the documented sort key;
\texttt{click/types.py:269} converts the user's \texttt{ValueError} via
\texttt{self.fail}, which raises \texttt{UsageError} --- the exception escapes
through a call rather than a \texttt{raise} statement, a shape no callee-local
rule can see; \texttt{pydantic/v1/\_hypothesis\_plugin.py:125} is a
\texttt{contextlib.suppress(Exception)} digit search in hypothesis test-support
code, confirming the SIM105-adjacent blind spot of Section~\ref{sec:rq1} from the
other side.

\paragraph{A fourth intra-procedural undecidability instance.}
\texttt{connectionpool.py:812} names a new false-positive mechanism:
\textbf{state-flag amplification}. The finding is \texttt{clean\_exit = False} (set
alongside \texttt{release\_this\_conn = False}), a control-flow flag consumed in
\texttt{finally} and after the handler; the \texttt{amplifies} criterion (``the
value outlives the call'') promotes it to HIGH exactly as it would a substituted
return value. Telling the two apart requires knowing where the variable is
consumed, which lies outside the handler. Like the three constructed
counterexamples and the \texttt{exception\_escapes} case below, it is documented
here and left in the code: each is an instance of the limit the paper argues,
and patching them would weaken the evidence they constitute.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{A detector this imprecise is still useful, but not as an oracle.}
On our corpus a maintainer following up every finding would read 621 handlers to
reach ${\sim}120$ genuine defects (84--134), essentially all of them three
copy-paste families in a single package. That 120 is a cross-frame extrapolation
rather than a measurement, and a deliberately package-aware one: it applies
\texttt{deepteam}'s labelled rate (12/14) to its 140 findings and counts the other
seven packages as zero, because Table~\ref{tab:bypackage} gives no warrant for
spreading a global share across them. It also inherits the clustering problem of
Section~\ref{sec:rq2} in full: \texttt{deepteam}'s 12/14 is twelve observations
that are \emph{three} code patterns, so the ${\sim}120$ is a projected count of
copies rather than of distinct bugs, and 84--134 is an interval on that copy count.
We leave the figure standing rather than deflating it by a factor we cannot derive
--- the copies would still be found and still have to be read, which is the cost
claim being made here --- but the replication factor on the labelled sample is four
(twelve observations, three patterns), and any reader who takes ${\sim}120$ as a
yield of distinct defects should divide by something of that order. The ${\sim}93$
that a global 15.0\% would
imply is exactly the homogeneous extrapolation that the per-package breakdown
rules out. The 15.0\% share
is itself measured on the v0.7.0 sample frame while the 621 count is v0.8.0, and
Section~\ref{sec:threats} carries the caveat. As an \emph{alarm}, that is
unusable. As a \emph{search-space reducer} it is a different proposition --- but the
honest comparison is then not against the corpus, it is against a cheaper reducer.
The eight pinned sdists contain 563{,}270 lines, of which 524{,}229 sit inside the
scan roots the detector actually reads and produces the 621 from. Against those
524{,}229 lines the detector's reduction is a factor of 844 (2.93 orders of
magnitude); the 907 that 563{,}270 would give pairs the findings with lines the
detector never read. \texttt{flake8 --select E722} reduces the \emph{same} 524{,}229
lines to 134 candidates --- a factor of 3{,}912, or 3.59 orders --- and contains all
12 confirmed defects. Measured against that, the detector's list is
\textbf{4.63$\times$ longer at equal recall} on the labelled defects, and the single
labelled defect the E722 list misses is one narrow-typed case that
Section~\ref{sec:rq5} declines to claim as an increment. So the reduction the tool
contributes is real against a maintainer reading the corpus and negative against a
maintainer running one linter flag. And a
reading of 80 sampled handlers found a systematic fail-open security verdict in a
red-teaming tool --- which is the actual argument that this family is worth a look,
and it does not require this detector to be the thing that looks.

\paragraph{The triangulation reframes the negative precision result --- as a
non-identifiability result.}
15\% defects --- 4.4\% once the three replicated families are counted as the three
patterns they are --- is bad for an alarm and unremarkable for a search tool; the
interesting fact is that the family's defining property --- silence --- also
disables the usual external check on the number. Spontaneous repair of tracked
family sites was 2.7\%; when the author surfaced adjudicated defect candidates,
acceptance was 37.2\%. These numbers measure different populations and cannot
be combined into a verdict (Section~\ref{sec:tri}) --- but not for the reason an
earlier version of this paper gave. The interval comparison is not fragile: 10 of
the 12 defensible rebasings of arm 2 leave the intervals disjoint, and the two that
do not are both the unweighted 15.0\% share, which the sampling design and the
package breakdown alike discredit. The comparison fails for a plainer reason. Its
three arms count three different kinds of object --- labelled findings, tracked
sites of which only 38.7\% carry a \texttt{failroute} rule at all, and
author-selected pull requests --- so there is no population whose rate all three
estimate. That is a limit of the data and not a theorem about the family, which is
why we call it \emph{non-identifiability} rather than impossibility: the paper can
name the observation that would identify it. What the triangulation establishes is
narrower: for this family neither spontaneous
behaviour nor triangulation around it can validate a detector; the reason is
quantifiable (a two-dimensional sensitivity,
Table~\ref{tab:sens}); and the missing datum is nameable --- maintainer
reaction to a random sample of findings.

\paragraph{Yield tracks codebase maturity, not rule design.}
The strongest predictor of whether a finding is a defect was not \emph{which}
rule fired but \emph{which package} it fired in. The mature, heavily-reviewed
packages had essentially no defects and a large number of carefully-commented
intentional contracts; the one package with a systematic problem had it
replicated across dozens of near-identical files. This suggests triage effort
should be allocated per-package after a cheap pilot sample, not uniformly.

\paragraph{Four layers of overfitting, stated against ourselves.}
The refactor's gains should be read as an overfitting report, not a quality
certificate. First, the acceptance gates demand no-regression on the same
corpus the patches were developed against, so every patch is fitted to its own
test set by construction. Second, all 140 labels (80 + 60) were available
during development; there is no held-out set, and the 60 supplementary labels
were drawn precisely from sites whose status the refactor changed. Third, every
counterexample that moved the argument --- the three constructed probes below,
the state-flag mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:control}, the mapping defects of
Section~\ref{sec:rq5} --- arrived from outside review; self-inspection caught
none of them. Fourth, and it is the terminal one: callee-local analysis cannot
define ``the caller cannot distinguish'' without caller intent, so its
separation criterion is necessarily a syntactic proxy, and proxies fitted on one
style fail on the next. The control corpus is the check we should have built
first: it finds no defect the tool can uniquely claim, on either style.

\paragraph{The conclusion the evidence supports.}
Failure-routing is an \emph{inter-procedural contract mismatch}: whether a
caught exception substituted by a value is a defect depends on what the caller
is entitled to assume, and that assumption lives outside the handler. Any
heuristic confined to the callee therefore degrades, without caller intent,
into fitting the shapes of whatever corpus it was tuned on. The call-site
evidence for a cross-procedural rescue is too thin to change that verdict: a
textual same-package call search over the 267 distinct flagged functions finds
at least one candidate call site for 153 (57\%), median 1; even counting every
bare textual mention, including comments and string literals, reaches only
208/267 (78\%) with median 2. The reachable sites exist, but at one or two
mentions each they carry no usable statement of intent --- the problem is
semantic density, not reachability. \texttt{tools/compute\_intervals.py
--control} recomputes the control cells. The call-site counts are the one
exception to this paper's reproduction rule: they came from ad-hoc scripts that
were not kept, so unlike every other number here they cannot be re-derived from
the repository. We state them as audit figures rather than claims, and no
conclusion rests on them.

\paragraph{Three constructed probes that pin the boundary --- code frozen.}
External review supplied three minimal probes, verified against the frozen
0.9.1 build on 2026-09-06: \texttt{is\_admin() -> bool} with
\texttt{except DatabaseTimeout: return False} is missed (a specific-typed
handler in a boolean probe is excused even when the caught exception is
operational); \texttt{fetch\_cached\_token() -> Optional[T]} with
\texttt{except RedisConnectionError: return None} is missed (a declared
\texttt{Optional} excuses hiding an infrastructure failure as a legitimate
absence); and a validation loop with the \texttt{try} \emph{outside} the
\texttt{for} and \texttt{except ValueError: return False} is reported at HIGH
(a short-circuit validation idiom flagged as failure-routing). All three live
in \texttt{Threats}: they are the sharpest statement of the
intra-procedural limit, and fixing them would trade evidence for comfort. The
same holds for the \texttt{exception\_escapes} case
(\texttt{realtime/\_session.py:2156}, where the exception object travels inside
the returned value) and the state-flag amplification of
Section~\ref{sec:control}: four known imprecisions, documented in the changelog
of the frozen 0.9.1 rather than patched.

\paragraph{The \texttt{contextlib.suppress} blind spot is real.}
0/19 of the findings are visible to the four selected rule sets, and none is
reported by \texttt{ruff} with all 969 of its rules enabled or by \texttt{pylint}
with its full default message set either; a popular lint rule
actively drives code toward that shape. Only a semgrep rule we wrote reaches
them (18/19).

\paragraph{Adoption, honestly.}
The tool is published on PyPI as \texttt{failroute}; at the 2026-09-01
measurement freeze it had \textbf{1 star and 0 forks}. We claim no adoption and
no impact beyond the measurements in this paper; the maintainer-behaviour dataset
and the triangulation method are the contributions we ask the reader to weigh.

\paragraph{On ``tool proposes, human adjudicates''.}
We report a case where we ran the detector over a well-maintained red-teaming
framework, sampled twelve semantic findings, traced each, concluded all twelve
were deliberate contracts, and \emph{filed nothing}. Given the base rate measured
here --- 15\% unweighted, 20.0\% weighted by the sampling design --- that outcome
is what the numbers predict, not an anomaly.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

The first five items are the limitations the triangulation verdict in
Section~\ref{sec:tri} depends on, and are cited there by name; the rest are the
study's other threats.

\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Limitation 1 --- the labelling arm is LLM-judged.} Both labelling
  rounds were performed by LLM agents, not human raters; the rounds agreed on
  80/80 items, which we read as \emph{shared bias of one model family, not as
  inter-rater reliability}. No Cohen's $\kappa$ is possible between two runs of
  the same rater species. All arm-1 proportions inherit this limit. The committed
  artefacts record the labels and the rationales but not the model identity or
  version, so a third party cannot re-run the labelling bit for bit. We were not
  able to recover that identity and therefore do not name a model: every ``defect
  rate'' in this paper is an LLM-adjudicated rate under an unrecoverable model
  configuration, and the weighted variants of it inherit exactly that limit.
\item \textbf{Limitation 2 --- SURVIVED is ambiguous in this family.} In the
  maintainer-behaviour dataset, SURVIVED can only be read as ``nobody acted'';
  it can \emph{not} be read as ``maintainers consider it a deliberate
  contract'', because for silent defects the two are observationally identical.
  Any number derived from arm 2 inherits this reading restriction.
\item \textbf{Limitation 3 --- arm-3 selection bias is irreducible.} The author
  chose which defects to report as PRs; acceptance (16/43) applies only to that
  self-selected set and cannot be extrapolated to the detector's findings.
  Reporting only merged PRs would have made this worse; the 27 closed-unmerged
  family PRs are counted in the denominator and are itemised, with closure
  reasons, in \texttt{paper/h1\_arm3\_classification.csv} (copied from the
  unreleased companion repository so the composition is checkable). Both ledgers
  are snapshots of the 2026-09-01 freeze rather than live queries: the author has
  merged further pull requests since, so a reader who re-runs the count today gets
  a larger denominator and a set of adjudications that does not include them.
  Three counting caveats: one of the 8 non-self-closures was a \emph{bot} action,
  not a maintainer judgement; two of the 27 are duplicates of other submissions
  and one more shares its defect substance with a counted merge, so the 43 PRs
  overcount distinct defects; and 8 of the 16 merged fixes lie in packages
  outside the tracked eight --- arm 3's population is not arm 2's. On the
  distinct-defect denominator those three rows come out, leaving 40 defects and an
  acceptance of 16/40 $=$ 40.0\% (26.3--55.4). We keep 16/43 as the headline
  because each of the 43 is a separate maintainer decision, and state 16/40 beside
  it so that neither reading has to be inferred.
\item \textbf{Limitation 4 --- the triangulation's arms are not
  commensurable.} Arm 1 counts labelled findings, arm 2 counts tracked sites, arm 3
  counts author-selected defect candidates, and 8 of arm 3's 16 merged candidates
  lie outside the tracked packages. No rate is common to the three, so the interval
  comparison supports no population-level verdict however it is rebased; we report
  directional consistency only.
\item \textbf{Limitation 5 --- arm 2's denominator is a convention, and its
  population is not the detector's.} The pipeline tracked 2{,}128 sites and labelled
  478 of them, discarding 1{,}650 (77.5\%) as too young (924), vanished (681) or
  mutated (45); nothing in the data selects among the resulting risk sets
  (Table~\ref{tab:sens}). Its tracking criterion --- a handler carrying no raise, log
  or warn signal --- omits the detector's three exemption gates and its
  fallback-value analysis, and never considers \texttt{contextlib.suppress} at all,
  so only 185 of the 478 rows (38.7\%) carry a \texttt{failroute} rule. Arm 1's
  defect share is measured on findings and does not transfer to that population,
  which is why every cell of Table~\ref{tab:sens} is a counterfactual rather than an
  estimate.
\item \textbf{Sample size.} $n=80$ of the v0.7.0 frame (659). Per-rule and
  per-package cells are small (\texttt{masked-exception} $n=1$). Wilson
  intervals are reported throughout instead of bare point estimates.
\item \textbf{Sample frame changed under the sample.} The draw came from
  v0.7.0's 659; 72/80 coordinates survive in v0.8.0's 621, and the deletions
  removed zero labelled DEFECTs --- but the surviving 72 are no longer a random
  sample of the current frame. Label proportions are stated for the v0.7.0
  frame only.
\item \textbf{Package selection is not random.} The eight packages were chosen
  because the author had prior contribution history in them. \texttt{deepteam} in
  particular is a package where the author had \emph{already found} a defect
  family, which inflates the overall defect rate. The seven-package 0/66 figure is
  the more conservative read.
\item \textbf{Recall ground truth is authored by the same person.} The 16 merged
  family fixes were written and submitted by the author; a maintainer merge
  records that the change was acceptable to the project --- it is not a
  maintainer finding that the pre-fix behaviour was a defect, and it says
  nothing about representativeness.
  The family adjudication of all 75 merged PRs was done by reading diffs (52
  earlier title-only judgements were re-verified from diffs in a dedicated
  pass, with 3 corrections).
\item \textbf{Co-location $\neq$ equivalent detection}
  (Section~\ref{sec:rq1} caveat). Reported conservatively as an upper bound on
  baseline coverage.
\item \textbf{The fifth baseline tool is ours.} The semgrep rule counted in the
  five-tool union was written by the author; coverage attributed to it is
  self-supplied.
\item \textbf{Construct validity (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).} The detector
  operationalises the except-handler subfamily only; 64.3\% of ground-truth
  defects lie outside it. All prevalence/recall claims are scoped to the
  subfamily.
\item \textbf{Control scope is two styles, not the ecosystem.}
  The control comparison covers a zero-bare-\texttt{except} infrastructure style
  against the main AI/ML style; both are Python, both are PyPI. The conclusion
  carried by Section~\ref{sec:control} is scoped to those two corpora, this
  detector build, and this adjudication protocol. A third style, another
  detector, or another annotator could move it, and the 7.6\% bound is over
  findings, not over the defect prevalence of disciplined code at large.
\item \textbf{Control adjudication is single-annotator.} The 38 findings and
  the 91 recall-side handlers were read once, by one annotator, with history,
  issue-record and test evidence taken only for the 2 unique HIGH findings;
  the remaining 36 + 91 rest on in-source contract evidence (docstrings,
  guarded read sites, explicit fallback comments, re-raise paths). Borderline
  CONTRACT-vs-DEFECT calls were resolved toward CONTRACT wherever a documented
  policy existed; the direction of that bias is stated so a re-adjudication
  can test it.
\item \textbf{Call-site counts are method-sensitive.}
  The 57\% / median-1 and 78\% / median-2 figures in Discussion are two
  textual search methods over the same 267 functions; an AST name-load count
  gives 33\% with median 0. All three agree the evidence is thin, but none is
  a call graph, and dynamic dispatch, re-exports and cross-package callers are
  uncounted throughout.
\item \textbf{Known imprecisions are frozen, not fixed.}
  The three constructed probes, the state-flag amplification
  (Section~\ref{sec:control}) and the \texttt{exception\_escapes} case
  (Section~\ref{sec:rq5}) are documented failure modes of the shipped 0.9.1
  predicate, and the dead-comparison family of the next item is a fifth. Any
  precision figure in this paper is net of the first four.
\item \textbf{Dead-comparison family: the rule fired on the right site for the
  wrong reason.} Section~\ref{sec:rq2} describes family 2 as a bare
  \texttt{except:} assigning \texttt{self.success = False} around a \texttt{try}
  body that holds the single discarded comparison \texttt{self.score == 1}. We
  re-read all five of those sites in the pinned corpus
  (\texttt{metrics/cross\_context\_retrieval},
  \texttt{external\_system\_abuse}, \texttt{hijacking}, \texttt{rbac},
  \texttt{shell\_injection}) and confirm the three facts the committed rationale
  records. A discarded comparison has no side effect, and it raised nothing for
  any value we placed in \texttt{self.score} --- \texttt{None}, \texttt{int},
  \texttt{float}, \texttt{str}, \texttt{bool}, tuple, list, \texttt{ndarray} ---
  so the handler is unreachable and \emph{no failure is converted at these five
  sites}. \texttt{silent-fallback} fired anyway: it reads the handler's shape
  (assigns a constant, does not re-raise) and carries no reachability gate, so it
  located a genuinely broken function and attributed the wrong mechanism to it.
  What is broken at the site is a missing assignment --- each of the five files
  states the intended form twice, in \texttt{measure} and in \texttt{a\_measure},
  which is why \texttt{is\_successful}'s \texttt{else} branch is a no-op that
  returns whatever those two left behind. The DEFECT reading survives, because
  the site is not a documented contract, but it survives on the missing
  assignment: the ``converted failure produces a wrong caller-visible outcome''
  half of the definition is not demonstrated for these five, and a reader who
  requires the stated mechanism to hold should treat the mechanism as established
  for the other seven and as unestablished for these. Unlike the four imprecisions
  above, this one sits inside the labelled DEFECTs rather than outside them, so no
  precision figure in this paper is net of it --- the five are counted as true
  positives, and on the reading just described they are true positives about a
  different defect than the rule names. Two further checks bound the impact
  without resolving it. \texttt{is\_successful} has no caller anywhere in the
  pinned sdist, so no in-package path exercises it; and the value
  \texttt{self.success} holds before either \texttt{measure} runs is set by
  \texttt{deepeval}'s \texttt{BaseMetric}, which is neither vendored in the corpus
  nor installed in the reproduction environment, so the never-measured path is
  unverified rather than benign. The other two families are unaffected: family 1's
  handler guards \texttt{model.get\_system\_prompt()} on a parameter typed
  \texttt{Optional}, and family 3's guards an attribute access and an ordering
  comparison, \texttt{metric\_data.score < 1}; both raise \texttt{AttributeError}
  for values those parameters accept, so in those seven the conversion mechanism is
  the one the label states. We did not re-adjudicate the five: the labels and their
  rationales are the committed artefact, and this item records the correction
  instead of acting on it.
\item \textbf{Version pinning is a snapshot.} All findings are relative to the
  pinned versions in \texttt{paper/\allowbreak corpus-lock.json};\allowbreak{} upstream may have fixed or
  introduced instances since.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Failure handling as an empirical object.}
Exception handling has a long empirical literature in statically typed languages:
Cabral and Marques' field study of Java and .NET~\cite{cabral2007} documented how
often exceptions are caught and swallowed; Sawadpong et
al.~\cite{sawadpong2012} characterised exception-handling \emph{defects}; and de
P\'adua and Shang quantified the prevalence of named exception-handling
anti-patterns over a large corpus~\cite{depadua2017prevalence} and revisited
developer practice through exception-flow analysis~\cite{depadua2017flow}. That
line of work asks \emph{whether} anti-patterns occur and \emph{how often}, and it
is our closest neighbour: we inherit its premise that handling shape is
measurable, and change the question. On a corpus of Python AI/ML packages, the
same syntactic
shapes that read as anti-patterns are predominantly \emph{intended contracts} ---
in our stratified sample of 80 sites, 80.0\% carried a contract reading and 5.0\%
were outright false positives, with the defect share concentrated almost entirely
in one package. A prevalence number without a per-site defect/contract breakdown
does not by itself support a triage decision; supplying that breakdown is what
the present measurement adds.

\paragraph{Why generic rules get muted.}
A second body of work explains the reception of such numbers. Developers report
false positives and noise as the leading reason they ignore or abandon static
analysis~\cite{johnson2013}; Ruthruff et al.\ made \emph{actionability} an
experimental target rather than a slogan~\cite{ruthruff2008}; Google's Tricorder
and FindBugs-Fixit programmes showed that an analysis only survives at scale when
it is filtered, prioritised and paired with a suppression
path~\cite{sadowski2015,ayewah2010,sadowski2018}. User-centred and follow-up
studies reach the same ordering from the other side: what developers need is a
warning they can act on~\cite{do2022,yedida2023}, and the mitigation of false
positives remains an open problem rather than a solved one~\cite{guo2023}.
WarningsGuru operationalised relevance with statistical bug
models~\cite{querel2018}, and Hu et al.\ recently measured the other end of the
same pipe --- which warnings developers actually suppress, and on what
grounds~\cite{hu2025suppressed}. Crisan and McNutt frame linting as a
sociotechnical activity~\cite{crisan2025}. Our contribution is not another
relevance filter: it is a \emph{semantic} account of why a particular family of
syntactic matches is disproportionately non-actionable, so that a suppression
decision can be justified by code shape rather than by developer fatigue.

\paragraph{Silent failure in machine-learning systems.}
The systems literature supplies the stakes. Sculley et al.\ named the pipeline
coupling and glue-code debt that makes ML behaviour brittle~\cite{sculley2015};
Polyzotis et al.\ proposed continuous data validation as the corrective at the
data boundary~\cite{polyzotis2019}; the ML Test Score encoded a rubric that tests
the pipeline rather than the model alone~\cite{breck2017}; Amershi et al.\ and
Paleyes et al.\
documented that the failures arrive at integration and deployment time, not
training time~\cite{amershi2019,paleyes2022}; Sambasivan et al.\ traced how a data
problem cascades through a high-stakes system until it surfaces as an unexplained
outcome~\cite{sambasivan2021}; and Dixit reported silent data corruption at scale
in production hardware, where no exception is raised at all~\cite{dixit2023}.
Zhang et al.\ position testing and robustness techniques across that same
spectrum~\cite{zhang2022}.

\paragraph{Increment over the two closest studies.}
Read side by side, de P\'adua and Shang~\cite{depadua2017prevalence} and Hu et
al.~\cite{hu2025suppressed} bound the problem on either side: the first measures
how often a handling shape appears, the second measures what developers do once a
tool points at it. Neither asks, \emph{per site}, whether the flagged construct is
a defect, an intended contract, or a tool artefact --- and neither reports what a
purely syntactic linter fails to see at all. This paper supplies both measurements
for Python AI/ML code: a labelled 80-site sample (12 DEFECT / 64 CONTRACT / 4
FALSE POSITIVE, with the defect mass concentrated in one package), and a
cross-tool comparison showing that four widely used syntactic linters jointly
cover 41.1\% of the 621 routed-failure sites we detect, with one entire pattern
--- \texttt{contextlib.suppress}-style silent suppression --- at 0/19 to shipped
rules. The
increment is therefore empirical and narrow: the \emph{meaning} of a flagged
handler, and the \emph{coverage boundary} of the tools practitioners actually run.

\section{Conclusion}

Except-handler failure-routing is a real and prevalent shape in AI/ML Python:
621 instances in the 524k lines the detector actually reads, 58.9\% of them outside
the reach of four mainstream syntactic linters, and none of the 19
\texttt{contextlib.suppress} variants reached by any rule those four ship or by
\texttt{ruff}'s full 969-rule set. But prevalence is not defectiveness, and the
defectiveness we could measure is both smaller and narrower than the prevalence. In
an LLM-labelled stratified sample 15\% were genuine defects and 80\% were
deliberate contracts, with the defects concentrated entirely in a single package;
weighting that sample by its own design raises the share to 20.0\%, and clustering
it by the code identity the defects actually have --- three replicated patterns ---
lowers it to \textbf{4.4\%} (Wilson 1.5--12.2). We quote the clustered figure as the
estimate and the 15\% as description, because the 12 defects are copies and a Wilson
interval on 12/80 treats them as independent. Every one of them sits in a bare
\texttt{except:}; across the 46 narrow-typed handlers in the same sample the defect
count is zero, one-sided upper bound 6.3\%.

The negative result is the one we did not set out to get. Replacing the detector's
predicate with the criterion its own definition states --- not ``returned a
constant'' but ``converted the failure into a value the caller cannot distinguish
from success, where that conversion is not the function's contract'' --- cut the
findings from 621 to 476 and \emph{raised} the share four off-the-shelf linters
already reach, from 41.1\% to 52.9\%. Over 140 labels the findings no linter reaches
carry 1 defect in 56 (1.8\%, 0.3--9.4) while the findings linters do reach carry 12
in 48 (25.0\%, 14.9--38.8). The layer built to make that distinction ---
exception/predicate isomorphism --- classified \emph{no} finding as isomorphic, so
it contributed nothing to the separation. What the refactor bought is real and
narrower than advertised: it kept all 13 labelled defects at HIGH and removed or
downgraded 92.1\% of the contracts whose function declares a None-admitting return
type, but three of those still report at HIGH or MEDIUM, and one labelled false
positive survives at HIGH and is reported twice. And \texttt{flake8 --select E722}
lists 134 candidates over the same corpus containing all 12 confirmed defects: a
reading task 4.63$\times$ shorter than the detector's 621 at equal recall on those
labels, and 3.55$\times$ shorter than the refactored 476.

The methodological contribution is a quantified non-identifiability result. For a
defect family whose defining property is silence, ``did maintainers fix it?''
is a broken validation instrument --- spontaneous repair of tracked family
sites was 2.7\% --- and the natural workaround fails too: triangulating
against maintainer reaction when told (37.2\% acceptance of author-reported
candidates) compares populations that are not merely differently selected but
differently constituted, so no rate is common to the three arms. The interval
disjointness that appears to support the ``nobody noticed'' reading is in fact
robust --- it survives 10 of the 12 defensible rebasings of arm 2 --- and is still
worth nothing, because the denominators do not count the same objects, and there
is no matched-population substitute. What remains is
honest but narrower: directionally consistent observations, a closure record
in which maintainer adjudication of surfaced family defects was favourable in
18 of 24 outcomes but adverse on the merits in two (an unresolved reachability
challenge and a scope decline), and an
open question whose missing datum we name --- maintainer reaction to a random
sample of findings. And the recall audit supplies the honest
boundary: 3 of 14 ground-truth defects detected, and 64.3\% of them route the
failure outside any handler --- the phenomenon is larger than the subfamily we,
and every handler-shaped detector, can see.

We think the useful conclusion is neither ``this class is undetectable'' nor ``our
tool detects it''. It is that the cheap syntactic rule and the expensive semantic
detector disagree about \emph{which} handlers matter, and on our labels the cheap
one is right more often: the property that separated defects from deliberate
contracts in this corpus was whether the handler is bare, and that is a one-flag
lint rule. Detectors for this family should therefore be evaluated against the
trivial baseline \emph{before} they are evaluated against the corpus --- a
reduction factor quoted over lines of code measures the corpus, not the tool --- and
should be deployed as search-space reducers with an explicit human adjudication step
only where an increment over that baseline has been measured. On the labels we have,
ours has not been: one case, which Section~\ref{sec:rq5} declines to generalise from.
The labelling pass that establishes this should still be run early. In our case it
was the fastest way to find a bug in the detector, and then the fastest way to find
that the refinement built to fix the bug's underlying imprecision did not help.

\paragraph{Two things a reproducer must know.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item The commands in this section all carry \texttt{PYTHONPATH=src}, and one of
  the reasons we used to give for that is wrong. This paragraph previously said an
  \emph{editable} install fails when the checkout path contains non-ASCII
  characters because the generated \texttt{.pth} pointer is mangled. That was tested
  during the work behind Section~\ref{sec:rq5} and does not reproduce: the path's
  encoding, the build backend and \texttt{setuptools} are all irrelevant to it. What
  actually happened on the authors' machine is that the \texttt{.pth} the editable
  install generated carried the macOS \texttt{UF\_HIDDEN} BSD flag, and CPython's
  \texttt{site.addpackage} (\texttt{site.py:176}) silently skips hidden files, so
  \texttt{import failroute} failed with no diagnostic pointing at the cause.
  Clearing the flag (\texttt{chflags nohidden}) fixed it; switching build backend
  did not. This is a property of one filesystem and not of the package, so a fresh
  clone on another machine will not have it --- which is exactly why it is worth
  recording: the failure is silent and the plausible explanation is wrong.
  \texttt{PYTHONPATH=src} is correct before and after that fix and on any machine,
  so the commands keep it.
\item The scanner exits with status 1 whenever it finds anything, even when the
  run succeeded and the export was written. Under \texttt{set -e} or in CI, a
  successful scan looks like a failure. The refactored detector of
  Section~\ref{sec:rq5} replaces this with a severity gate (\texttt{--fail-on},
  defaulting to \texttt{high}, plus \texttt{--exit-zero}), and exits 2 on an
  internal scan error; the behaviour described here is the pinned version's.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Availability and ethics.} The corpus is eight publicly distributed PyPI
source distributions, each used under its own licence; nothing was taken from a
private source. The maintainer-behaviour ground truth is a set of public GitHub
issue and pull-request records, every one cited by its public number, and the two
datasets derived from them are committed to the public repository and itemised in
the list above, so that the classification can be audited row by row. The study
analyses publicly recorded artefacts of professional work on public repositories,
and uses no non-public data about identifiable individuals. The author's conflict of
interest --- writing both the detector under evaluation and one of the five
baselines --- is disclosed on the title page, and the results unfavourable to the
tool are reported alongside the favourable ones.
